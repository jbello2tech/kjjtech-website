Home VPN: How to Make Your Traffic Show Your Home IP (UniFi & GL.iNet)

By Jorge Bello, founder · Nov 23, 2026 · 11 min read

Most VPN ads promise to hide where you are. A lot of people actually want the opposite: when they're in a hotel, an Airbnb or a coffee shop, they want their laptop, phone and TV to behave exactly as if they were sitting at home, on their own internet connection, with their own public IP address.

That's what a home VPN does. Instead of paying a VPN company to route you through one of their data centers, you build an encrypted tunnel back to your own router, and everything you do on the road exits through your home internet. This guide covers how to do it with UniFi, and with GL.iNet travel routers like the Beryl 7 and the 5G Mudi 7.

Why Your Home IP Matters

Websites and apps judge you partly by the IP address you connect from. A home internet connection has a residential IP from your ISP. Commercial VPN services give you a shared data center IP that thousands of other people are using, and many services know it. Reasons people want their home IP while traveling:

  • Fewer fraud flags. Banks, card issuers and brokerage sites are far less likely to lock your account or demand extra verification when you log in from the same IP you always use.
  • Work tools with IP allowlists. Remote desktops, admin portals and some SaaS tools only accept connections from approved addresses. If your home IP is approved, you're in.
  • Your own subscriptions and local content. Streaming and local TV services tied to your household or region work the same as they do at home.
  • Safer public Wi-Fi. Everything is encrypted from your device (or travel router) all the way home, so the hotel network sees nothing but one encrypted connection.
  • Access to your home network. Cameras, a NAS, printers and smart-home controls are reachable as if you were on the couch.

How a Home VPN Works

Your home router runs a VPN server. Your laptop, phone, or a travel router runs a VPN client that connects back to it. We recommend WireGuard: it's fast, built into UniFi and GL.iNet, and reconnects almost instantly when you switch networks.

The key setting is a full tunnel. In WireGuard terms, the client's AllowedIPs is 0.0.0.0/0, ::/0, meaning all traffic goes through the tunnel, not just traffic for your home devices. UniFi's WireGuard client configs are full-tunnel by default. A split tunnel would let you reach your home devices but still show the hotel's IP to everything else.

What you need at home

  • A router that can be a VPN server. Any UniFi gateway or Cloud Gateway (UDM, UDM Pro, UDM SE, UCG Ultra / Max / Fiber, UXG) works. No UniFi? A GL.iNet router or a Tailscale exit node can do the same job (covered below).
  • A reachable public IP, or Teleport/Tailscale. A normal WireGuard server needs a public IP on your router's WAN. Some ISPs (especially fixed wireless, Starlink and 5G home internet) use CGNAT, which blocks incoming connections.
  • Decent upload speed. On the road, your home's upload becomes your download. 20–50 Mbps upload is fine for browsing, video calls and HD streaming; fiber is ideal.

Quick CGNAT check

In UniFi, open the dashboard and note your WAN IP. Then visit whatismyipaddress.com from home. If the two match, you have a real public IP. If UniFi shows something starting with 100.64–100.127, 10., or 192.168., you're behind CGNAT or a second router. Ask your ISP for a public IP, put the ISP modem in bridge mode, or use Teleport or Tailscale, which work around it.

Option 1: The WireGuard Server on Your UniFi Gateway

This is the most flexible setup and the one we use most. It works with phones, laptops, and any travel router.

  1. In UniFi Network, go to Settings → VPN → VPN Server and click Create New.
  2. Choose WireGuard. Leave the port at UDP 51820 unless you have a reason to change it. UniFi opens the firewall for it automatically.
  3. If your gateway isn't the first device on your internet connection (an ISP router sits in front of it), forward UDP 51820 on that ISP router to your UniFi, or better, put the ISP device in bridge mode.
  4. Set up Dynamic DNS (Settings → Internet → your WAN → Dynamic DNS) so your clients find home even when your ISP changes your IP. Then set the server's address to that hostname.
  5. Under Clients, add one client per device (laptop, phone, travel router). Download the config file, or show the QR code to scan with the WireGuard app on a phone.
  6. Import it into the WireGuard app (or your travel router), connect, and check your IP. It should now show your home address.

One client per device matters: if a phone is lost, you delete just that client instead of re-keying everything.

Option 2: UniFi Teleport (No Port Forwarding)

Teleport is Ubiquiti's one-click VPN. It also uses WireGuard under the hood, but there are no config files: you generate an invite in Settings → VPN → Teleport, open it in the free WiFiman app on your phone or computer, and you're connected. Because Teleport handles the connection setup for you, it's the easiest option if you're behind CGNAT or don't want to touch port forwarding.

The trade-off: Teleport is app-based, so it covers the devices that can run WiFiman. For TVs, streaming sticks and work laptops you can't install apps on, use a travel router.

Option 3: A Travel Router (the Best Way to Look “Home”)

A travel router sits between the hotel network and your devices. It joins the hotel Wi-Fi (or plugs into the room's Ethernet jack), builds one WireGuard tunnel to your home UniFi, and broadcasts its own private Wi-Fi network. Every device that connects to it, including ones that can't run a VPN app, comes out the other end with your home IP.

Pro tip: give the travel router the same Wi-Fi name and password as your home network. Your phone, laptop, Fire TV and game console join it automatically, with no setup at every new hotel.

If your home network is UniFi, Ubiquiti's own UniFi Travel Router is the most seamless choice: you bind it to your UniFi site, pick which of your home Wi-Fi networks to copy, and it brings up a Teleport tunnel home. Setting up a GL.iNet router with your UniFi

  1. On your UniFi WireGuard server, add a new client named after the router (for example “Beryl 7”) and download its config file.
  2. On the GL.iNet admin page, go to VPN → WireGuard Client, add a new provider, and upload the config.
  3. Start the connection, then open the VPN Dashboard and turn on Block Non-VPN Traffic.
  4. Under Wireless, set the Wi-Fi name and password to match your home network.
  5. At the hotel, connect the GL.iNet to the hotel Wi-Fi (or the Mudi 7 to 5G), complete any login page, and your devices are home.

No UniFi at Home? Other Ways to Do It

  • A GL.iNet router at home. Desktop models like the Flint 2 and Flint 3 can run a WireGuard server, and pair neatly with a Beryl 7 or Mudi 7 on the road.
  • A Tailscale exit node. Install Tailscale on an always-on device at home (a mini PC, a Mac, an Apple TV, or a GL.iNet router) and enable it as an exit node. GL.iNet travel routers can use it too. Tailscale works behind CGNAT, which makes it the go-to for Starlink and 5G home internet.
  • Your ISP router. Some ISP and consumer routers have a built-in VPN server. It works, but options and speed vary widely.

Make It Airtight: Avoiding Leaks

A VPN that's “on” can still leak details that don't match your home connection. Check these:

  • DNS. Make sure DNS requests go through the tunnel to your home gateway, not to the hotel's DNS server. UniFi client configs point DNS at your gateway by default; keep it that way.
  • IPv6. If the hotel offers IPv6 and your tunnel only carries IPv4, some traffic can bypass the VPN. Either include ::/0 in the tunnel or turn IPv6 off on the travel router.
  • Tunnel drops. Use a kill switch (GL.iNet's Block Non-VPN Traffic, or “Block connections without VPN” on Android) so nothing slips out while the tunnel reconnects.
  • WebRTC in browsers. Video-calling features in browsers can reveal your local network addresses. With a travel router, there's nothing interesting to reveal.
  • Device location settings. Phones and laptops can also report location from GPS and nearby Wi-Fi, independent of your IP. A VPN changes your IP, not your physical location.

Test it

Once you're connected on the road, visit whatismyipaddress.com (should show your home IP and ISP), dnsleaktest.com (should show only your home ISP or your chosen DNS provider), and browserleaks.com/webrtc.

Using this for work? Check your employer's remote-work and travel policy first. Many companies have rules about working from other states or countries for tax, legal or security reasons, and a home IP doesn't change where you actually are. Done with your IT team's blessing, a home VPN is a great way to keep allowlisted tools working on the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a home VPN legal?

Yes. Connecting to your own network over an encrypted tunnel is the same thing businesses do every day for remote staff. Just follow the terms of the services you use and your employer's policies.

Will it slow my internet down?

Your speed on the road is limited by the slower of two things: the local connection (hotel Wi-Fi or 5G) and your home upload speed. WireGuard itself adds very little overhead, and routers like the Beryl 7 and Mudi 7 can encrypt far faster than most hotel Wi-Fi delivers.

Do I need a commercial VPN subscription?

No. A home VPN replaces it for this purpose. A commercial VPN is still useful if you want to appear somewhere other than home, but it can't give you your home IP.

Can I use the Beryl 7 or Mudi 7 without UniFi?

Yes. They work with any WireGuard or OpenVPN server, a GL.iNet router at home, or a Tailscale exit node.

What if my home internet goes down while I'm traveling?

The tunnel can't connect, and with a kill switch on, the travel router stops passing traffic. You can turn the VPN off on the GL.iNet temporarily. Putting your UniFi gateway and modem on a small UPS avoids most short outages.
